Get Useful Answers Out of AI Chatbots

7 min read · By Muhammad Zakria

Most disappointing AI answers come from vague questions, not bad AI. Chatbots like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini respond to the specificity you give them — a one-line question gets a generic one-line-quality answer. A few habits make the difference between "that's not quite what I meant" and getting it right on the first try.

Give it the context a colleague would need

If you asked a coworker "can you fix this?" with no other information, they'd ask clarifying questions before starting. A chatbot won't always ask — it'll guess, and often guess wrong. Include what you're trying to accomplish, any constraints (length, tone, audience, format), and relevant background. "Write an email declining a meeting" gets a generic result. "Write a short, friendly email declining a Tuesday meeting because of a scheduling conflict, and suggest Thursday instead" gets something you can actually send.

Ask for one thing, or clearly separated things

Chatbots handle a single clear request better than a bundled one. If you need three things — a summary, a list of action items, and a follow-up email — ask for them as three numbered items in your prompt, not as one run-on sentence. This isn't about being polite; it changes how the model organizes its response and reduces the chance it skips part of what you asked.

Show, don't just tell, when format matters

If you want a specific structure — a table, a certain tone, a particular length — showing a short example gets you there faster than describing it. "Write like this: [paste a paragraph in the voice you want]" works better than "write casually." If a previous response was close but not quite right, tell it specifically what to change rather than starting over: "Make this shorter and drop the third paragraph" gets a better result than a brand new prompt from scratch.

Use follow-ups instead of restarting

Chatbot conversations keep context, so treat it like an ongoing conversation, not a series of isolated searches. If the first answer is 80 percent right, refine it — "make the second point stronger" or "this is too formal, loosen it up" — rather than writing an entirely new prompt. This also applies to research: if an answer raises a follow-up question, just ask it directly instead of restating everything you already discussed.

Where chatbots are genuinely unreliable

AI chatbots can state incorrect facts with the same confident tone as correct ones — this is a well-documented limitation, not a rare glitch. Be especially cautious with: specific statistics or dates, citations and quotes (verify these independently before using them anywhere that matters), medical or legal specifics, and anything about very recent events, since most models have a training cutoff and may not know about things that happened after it. If a chatbot gives you a specific number, name, or quote you plan to rely on, verify it against an actual source before using it.

A useful habit: ask the chatbot to flag its own uncertainty. Adding "tell me if you're not confident about any part of this" to a factual question sometimes prompts a more honest answer, though it's not foolproof — the model can still be confidently wrong. Independent verification is still the only real safeguard for anything that matters.

Use it for drafts, not final answers, on anything important

AI is strong at generating a first draft, structuring your own scattered thoughts, or rewording something you already understand well enough to check. It's weaker as the sole source of truth for anything with real consequences — financial decisions, medical questions, legal language, or facts you'll repeat to someone else. Treat its output as a draft you're editing, not a finished answer you're forwarding unread.

Iterate on tone with direct instructions

If output feels too robotic, too casual, too long, or too hedging, say so directly: "cut the hedging language," "make this sound like a text message, not an email," "assume I already know the basics — skip the intro." These instructions carry over for the rest of that conversation, so you often only need to say them once per session rather than repeating them every message.

A quick before-you-send checklist

  • Did I give enough context that a stranger could act on this without guessing?
  • If there are facts, dates, or quotes involved, have I verified them independently?
  • Does the tone and length match what I actually need, or am I sending the first draft as-is?
  • For anything with real consequences, would I be comfortable if this were wrong?
