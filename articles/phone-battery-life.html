How to Extend Your Phone's Battery Life

7 min read · By Muhammad Zakria

Phone battery advice tends to repeat the same few tips without explaining which ones actually matter. Screen and network settings make the biggest difference; most of the rest is minor.

The biggest lever: screen brightness and timeout

The display is typically the single largest drain on a phone battery. Use auto-brightness rather than manually maxing it out, and shorten your screen timeout (Settings → Display) to 30 seconds if you don't need it longer — every extra minute the screen stays on unattended after you stop using it is wasted battery.

Check which apps are actually draining your battery

Both iPhone (Settings → Battery) and Android (Settings → Battery → Battery usage) show a breakdown of battery use by app over the last 24 hours or several days. Look for anything unexpectedly high — a rarely used app consuming a disproportionate share usually means it's running background tasks it shouldn't be, and is worth restricting or uninstalling.

Background app refresh and location access

Apps that refresh content in the background (social media, news apps) and apps with constant location access are common, hidden battery drains. On iPhone, Settings → General → Background App Refresh lets you disable this per app. On Android, Settings → Apps → [app] → Battery has an "unrestricted / optimized / restricted" toggle. You don't need to disable this for apps you use constantly, just the ones sitting idle most of the day.

Signal strength matters more than people realize

A phone working to maintain a weak cellular or Wi-Fi signal uses noticeably more battery than one with a strong, stable connection — this is why battery tends to drain faster in basements, elevators, or fringe coverage areas. If you're regularly in a weak-signal area, switching to Wi-Fi calling when available, or enabling airplane mode in truly dead zones, prevents the phone from continuously searching for a signal.

Push email and constant sync

Email set to "push" (instant delivery) keeps a persistent connection open and checks constantly. Switching to "fetch every 15/30/60 minutes" for accounts where instant delivery doesn't matter is a small but real battery saver, particularly with multiple email accounts syncing at once.

Low Power Mode / Battery Saver — use it more than you think

Both platforms' built-in power-saving modes (Low Power Mode on iPhone, Battery Saver on Android) reduce background activity and some visual effects in exchange for real battery gains, with minimal impact on everyday use. Many people only turn this on when the battery is already critically low — turning it on earlier in the day, especially on a day you know you'll be away from a charger, extends your buffer considerably.

A battery percentage that drops unusually fast right after an operating system update is common and often resolves within a few days, as the system reindexes and finishes background setup tasks. If poor battery life persists for more than a week after an update, check the battery-usage breakdown for a specific culprit rather than assuming the update itself is permanently at fault.

Myths worth retiring

  • "Force-closing all your apps saves battery." Modern phones manage backgrounded apps efficiently on their own; manually closing everything can actually cost more battery when apps have to fully reload from scratch.
  • "You must let it die completely sometimes." Not true for modern lithium batteries — regular deep discharges are harder on long-term battery health, not better for it.

Quick wins in order of impact

  • Use auto-brightness and a short screen timeout.
  • Check the battery-usage breakdown for unexpected drains.
  • Restrict background refresh and location access for apps that don't need it.
  • Use Wi-Fi calling or airplane mode in areas with weak signal.
  • Switch non-urgent email accounts from push to scheduled fetch.
  • Turn on Low Power Mode / Battery Saver proactively, not just when critical.
